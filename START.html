Situations and next steps

Power outageA power failure can also affect water, heating, phones, stores, and traffic signals.Show stepsClose ⌄

Basic step · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Check whether the outage is limited to your home. Stay away from downed wires and damaged electrical equipment.
  2. Use flashlights, a charged power bank, and a battery or hand-crank radio. Save your phone battery for important calls.
  3. Never use a generator, charcoal grill, or camp stove indoors. Run generators outside, at least 20 feet from doors, windows, and vents.
  4. Follow local utility and emergency instructions. Check refrigerated food safety after the outage.

Open as text fileSources: FEMA-POWER, CDC-POWER, NOAA-RADIO

EvacuationAn evacuation route or shelter can change. Current local instructions come first.Show stepsClose ⌄

Check current local guidance · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. If local officials tell you to leave, follow their route and timing. Do not wait for this offline card to update.
  2. Take needed medicine, ID, a small document folder, phone, charger, water, sturdy shoes, and supplies for children or pets if you can do so safely.
  3. Tell a trusted contact where you plan to go. Help neighbors who need assistance without placing yourself in danger.
  4. Return only when local officials say it is safe.

Open as text fileSources: FEMA-HAZARDS, NWS-HURRICANE, CDC-DOCS

Water and sanitationTap water may be unavailable or unsafe after a disaster.Show stepsClose ⌄

Check current local guidance · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Follow the exact notice from your local water utility or health department. A boil-water notice and a do-not-drink notice are different.
  2. Use bottled or otherwise confirmed safe water for drinking, food, brushing teeth, and baby formula when tap water is unsafe.
  3. Keep supplies for hand hygiene and sanitation. Plan for people with special medical or infant feeding needs.
  4. Do not judge water safety by its appearance or smell. Check local instructions before using tap water again.

Open as text fileSources: CDC-WATER, CDC-ADVISORY

Extreme heatFind a cooler place, reduce activity, and check on people at higher risk.Show stepsClose ⌄

Check current local guidance · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Follow National Weather Service heat alerts for your area. Avoid strenuous outdoor activity during the hottest hours.
  2. Drink water regularly unless a clinician has told you to limit fluids. Find shade or air conditioning.
  3. Check on older adults, children, and people with health conditions. Never leave people or pets in a parked car.
  4. For severe symptoms such as confusion or loss of consciousness, call 911.

Open as text fileSources: NWS-HEAT, NWS-HEAT-ALERTS, US-911

Winter storm and coldStay warm and dry while watching for local weather warnings.Show stepsClose ⌄

Check current local guidance · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Stay inside when travel is dangerous. If you must go out, dress in layers and keep exposed skin covered.
  2. Use approved heating equipment safely and follow ventilation instructions. Never heat a home with a charcoal grill, camp stove, or oven.
  3. Keep a flashlight, radio, blankets, and needed medicine available during power loss.
  4. Seek medical help for suspected hypothermia, frostbite, or sudden worsening of health.

Open as text fileSources: NWS-WINTER, FEMA-POWER, US-911

Flood and flash floodWater depth and road damage are hard to judge. Move to safer ground.Show stepsClose ⌄

Check current local guidance · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Follow local flood warnings and evacuation orders. Move to higher ground when flash flooding threatens.
  2. Do not walk or drive into floodwater, and never drive around a road barrier.
  3. Avoid flooded basements and electrical equipment. Turn off power only if it can be done safely.
  4. Return and clean up only when local officials say the area is safe.

Open as text fileSources: NWS-FLOOD, FEMA-HAZARDS

Alerts and communicationA phone alert is only one way to learn what is happening.Show stepsClose ⌄

Check current local guidance · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. When service works, check alerts from your local emergency management agency and National Weather Service.
  2. Wireless Emergency Alerts, radio/TV alerts, and NOAA Weather Radio may carry official warnings. Reception and coverage vary by location and equipment.
  3. Keep a paper contact list, a battery-powered radio, and a family meeting plan. An out-of-area contact can help reconnect people.
  4. If you cannot reach 911, look for locally announced alternatives; do not assume a nationwide backup number.

Open as text fileSources: FEMA-IPAWS, NOAA-RADIO, US-911, FEMA-PLAN

Medical help and first aidCall for emergency help, protect yourself, and use skills you have practiced.Show stepsClose ⌄

Check current local guidance · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Make sure the scene is safe for you before helping someone else.
  2. For an immediate police, fire, or medical emergency, call 911. Give your location and describe what happened. Stay on the line until the call-taker tells you to hang up.
  3. Give first aid within your training while waiting for responders. Keep a list of medicines and medical needs ready for your own household.
  4. Refresh first-aid training; a short card cannot replace hands-on practice.

Open as text fileSources: US-911, CDC-KIT

Documents and recordsKeep important originals protected and make readable backup copies.Show stepsClose ⌄

Basic step · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Collect the IDs, insurance information, medical records, and other documents that matter to your household.
  2. Put sensitive digital copies in a tested encrypted vault. Keep its password and recovery key separate from the drive.
  3. Store original documents safely; a digital copy may not satisfy a specific agency's requirements.
  4. Test opening a backup copy on another device and keep a second encrypted copy elsewhere.

Open as text fileSources: CDC-DOCS, CRYPTO-RECOVERY

Basic suppliesStart with a small kit you can actually use and maintain.Show stepsClose ⌄

Basic step · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Plan water, food, lights, a radio, chargers, medicines, and hygiene items around your household's needs.
  2. A common US planning baseline is at least three days of supplies; longer may be needed in some places or for specific needs.
  3. Include children, pets, accessibility needs, and a plan for leaving home quickly.
  4. Check dates, batteries, and your backup files regularly.

Open as text fileSources: FEMA-HAZARDS, CDC-KIT

Wildfire smokeSmoke can be harmful even far from a fire; local air quality and orders matter.Show stepsClose ⌄

Check current local guidance · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Check current local air quality and emergency orders when service is available. Leave immediately if officials order evacuation.
  2. If staying home is safe, reduce smoke entering rooms and use a cleaner-air room or appropriate air cleaner if available.
  3. If the home becomes too hot or smoky, seek a safer indoor location. Check on children and people with heart or lung conditions.
  4. Do not rely on a cloth mask to filter wildfire smoke.

Open as text fileSources: AIRNOW-SMOKE, CDC-SMOKE

TornadoA tornado warning for your area means act quickly.Show stepsClose ⌄

Check current local guidance · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Go to a basement, safe room, or small interior room on the lowest floor of a sturdy building, away from windows.
  2. Cover your head and neck. Follow the shelter plan at work or school.
  3. Mobile homes, tents, and vehicles are unsafe in a tornado. Reach a sturdy shelter if you can do so before it arrives.
  4. Keep listening to local alerts and NOAA Weather Radio if available.

Open as text fileSources: NWS-TORNADO, NOAA-RADIO

Hurricane and coastal stormCoastal risks and evacuation zones are local; plan before a storm arrives.Show stepsClose ⌄

Check current local guidance · reviewed 2026-11-09

  1. Check your local evacuation zone and route in advance. Follow local officials and leave immediately if ordered.
  2. Monitor National Weather Service and local emergency management information when available.
  3. Keep a go-bag with medicines, documents, water, and chargers ready. Protect your property only when it is safe to do so.
  4. If told to stay, use an interior room away from windows. Return after evacuation only when officials say it is safe.

Open as text fileSources: NWS-HURRICANE, FEMA-HAZARDS
